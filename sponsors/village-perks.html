What "quid pro quo" means here

Whenever a donor receives something of real value in exchange for a gift — a plate of food, an artwork, a session — the IRS calls this a "quid pro quo" contribution. Only the portion of the payment above the fair market value of what they received is tax-deductible.

The $75 disclosure rule

If the total payment is more than $75, we're required to give the donor a written disclosure stating (a) only the amount over the item's fair market value is deductible, and (b) a good-faith estimate of that value. Example: "Your $50 contribution includes a $12 plate of ital stew; $38 is tax-deductible." Below $75 total, this written disclosure isn't legally required, but it's good practice to include anyway.

Why the fair market value field matters

This is why every prospect in the tracker needs a real fair-market-value number, not just a suggested donation amount — without it we can't generate a correct receipt, and Zeffy's Online Shop needs both numbers to build the receipt automatically.

Inventory caps

One-of-a-kind items (original artwork, limited sessions) need a hard inventory cap so we never oversell a real, physical thing. Perishable items (a plate of food) should be capped to what the vendor can realistically produce for festival day.
