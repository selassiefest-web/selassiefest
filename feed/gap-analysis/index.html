Gap Analysis of the FEED Stakeholder Brief

What the predevelopment plan still needs, and where I can help as a volunteer.

Prepared by Albert Harris · September 2026 · Reviewed: FEED Junction Market Stakeholder Brief (Draft 2.4, September 2026) and feedchi.com

Overall read. The brief is strong on concept and unusually honest about what isn't known yet: it's clear there's no site control, no committed vendors and no SNAP approval. Its main weakness is that it describes what has to happen but not who does it, by when, or what success looks like in numbers. That's where volunteers can help most.

Gaps, most important first

  1. Site control is the critical path, and there's no plan for getting it

    • The space is offered as a sublease, and the master lease ends September 30, 2031. A fundable five-year plan would outlast the sublease, so FEED needs to know who the landlord is and whether a direct lease or an extension is possible.
    • No step in the plan names who will approach the landlord or master tenant, or what level of site control funders will require.
    Worth checking: the brief lists the building as "built in 2022." My recollection is that a Whole Foods opened at 63rd & Halsted around 2016. Confirm the date against the leasing brochure before the brief circulates further, since one wrong fact undercuts the brief's careful sourcing.

  2. The timeline conflicts with itself

    • Weeks 1–2 are for completing organizational formation, yet grant applications are due to go out by October 1.
    • Most grants require an existing nonprofit with IRS 501(c)(3) status, which takes months. The brief doesn't mention a fiscal sponsor, the usual way to apply for grants in the meantime.

  3. No names, owners or team

    • No task in the 30-day plan has an owner or a due date.
    • There's no board list and no named roles.
    • There's no volunteer structure, even though almost all of the pre-opening work (interviews, surveys, canvassing, Farm Day, sign-ups) depends on volunteers.

  4. Community validation has no method or targets

    • The brief lists what it will track but sets no numbers, such as how many interviews, how many sign-ups by opening, or how many survey responses.
    • It doesn't say where the data will be kept, or who can see it.
    • There's no written question set yet for the "structured interviews."

  5. Legal and tax questions are missing for the central-checkout model

    • Sales tax: who is the seller of record when FEED collects payment for many independent vendors?
    • Handling vendors' money: collecting funds on vendors' behalf and paying them out may bring payment-processing or money-handling rules into play.
    • City licensing and inspections: City of Chicago retail food licenses, Department of Public Health inspections for each vendor or for the market, and zoning.
    • Insurance: what each vendor must carry.

    SNAP is covered thoroughly; these aren't covered at all. They need a lawyer and an accountant.

  6. The vendor pipeline has no size or shape

    • The brief doesn't say how many vendors it takes to fill 18,141 square feet or to cover rent.
    • The vendor scorecard isn't slated until days 30–60.
    • The website shows a shared kitchen and cold chain that the brief doesn't mention, so the two documents disagree.

  7. The finances have no rough range at all

    • A final pro forma isn't expected yet, but there's no rent figure and no rough break-even (for example, "we need X vendors paying Y to cover occupancy"). Funders will ask for that first.

  8. Heavy reliance on one technology firm, with no terms

    • The whole operating platform depends on DirectiveIO, with no stated cost, data ownership, IP ownership or exit plan.
    • If anyone at DirectiveIO is connected to FEED's leadership, the conflict-of-interest policy should cover it.

  9. The coalition is thin

    • Only one partner is named (Common Ground Collective).
    • Nearby organizations worth approaching (verify each first):
      • Kennedy-King College's Washburne Culinary & Hospitality Institute, near 63rd & Halsted, for workforce training
      • Growing Home, which runs urban farms in Englewood, for farmers and produce
      • IMAN (Inner-City Muslim Action Network), which does food-access work on the South Side
      • The Greater Chicago Food Depository
      • Englewood community groups such as Teamwork Englewood and RAGE (Resident Association of Greater Englewood)

  10. The trade-area data doesn't support the culture claim yet

    • The brief promises vendors that reflect the cultures and nationalities of the neighborhood, but its data covers population, income and spending only. A breakdown of who lives in the trade area would back that promise.

  11. The website and documents are out of step

    • Website: feedchi.com shows renderings and a 3D tour but has no About or leadership page, no volunteer or donate page, and no statement that FEED doesn't control the site yet. Visitors may assume it's a done deal.
    • Version labels: the brief's cover says Draft 2, its footer says Draft 2.4, and the file was shared as Draft 3.
    • Asks of government: "What We Need" is a single paragraph with no specific asks.

What I'd suggest I take on first

I'm also leading the Bongo Beach Park effort, so I'd rather do two or three things well than spread thin:

  1. The sign-up and validation data system. Quick for me to build, and it closes gap 4 directly.
  2. Vendor intake and cultural vendor recruitment. It draws on a festival vendor network FEED doesn't have yet.
  3. Farm Day. Event production is what I do.

Both projects sit on the 63rd Street corridor, so events and sign-ups could support each other down the line.
