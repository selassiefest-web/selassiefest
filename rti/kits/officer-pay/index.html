Officer Pay Pathway

How Stephen Henry (President) and Paul Kelly (Vice President and Treasurer) could be paid for a possible role producing Full Spectrum and running its daily and weekly activities. It covers the IRS and Illinois rules that apply, and the board, accounting and legal setup that has to be in place first.

Bottom line

Viable

A 501(c)(3) may pay its own officers for real work at a reasonable rate. Nothing in federal or Illinois law bars it. Small nonprofits whose founders become their first staff do this all the time. Three things make it safe: the pay must be reasonable and backed by comparison data, it must be approved by directors with no stake in it, and it must be documented and reported.

The one real blocker

RTI has three directors, and two of them would be paid. Stephen can't vote on his own pay or on Paul's, and Paul can't vote on his own pay or on Stephen's. Approving each other's pay is a trade, which the IRS treats as a conflict. That leaves Albert as the only director without a conflict. Illinois law requires a majority of disinterested directors to approve a conflicted transaction, and as we read it, a single director can't approve one alone. So RTI needs at least two more independent directors before any pay vote. This is the first step and the most important one.

IRC 4958; Treas. Reg. 53.4958-6(c)(1)(iii) (reciprocal approval is a conflict); 805 ILCS 105/108.60 (director conflicts; confirm the single-director rule with counsel)
Fix what we've already told funders

Several current drafts say the officers are unpaid: the Seabury LOI ("Officers serve without pay, which keeps costs low"), the Springboard call notes ("Officers unpaid"), and the funder materials. Those are true today. Once pay is approved, change them to "Officers currently serve without pay" before submitting, and update them when pay starts. A funder that reads "unpaid" and then sees salaries on the 990 will ask why.

Federal (IRS) issues

1. Private inurement

No part of a 501(c)(3)'s net earnings may benefit insiders. Pay that is reasonable, for services actually performed, is not inurement. Pay that is excessive, disguised or unearned can cost RTI its exemption.

IRC 501(c)(3); Treas. Reg. 1.501(c)(3)-1(c)(2)

2. Excess benefit tax ("intermediate sanctions")

Stephen and Paul are disqualified persons because of their positions: the president and the treasurer are named roles in the regulation, and so are voting directors. Albert is one too. If either is paid more than the work is worth:

  • the person paid owes a 25% excise tax on the excess and must pay the excess back, or the tax becomes 200%;
  • any director who knowingly approves it owes 10% of the excess, up to $20,000 per transaction.

Disqualified-person status lasts five years after someone leaves the role, so stepping off the board doesn't remove these rules.

IRC 4958(a)–(b), (f)(1); Treas. Reg. 53.4958-3(a), (c)

3. The safe harbor: rebuttable presumption of reasonableness

If RTI follows three steps, the IRS has to prove the pay was unreasonable, not the other way around.

  1. Advance approval by the board or a board committee made up entirely of members with no conflict. Stephen and Paul leave the room for the discussion and the vote.
  2. Comparability data. What similar organizations pay for similar work. Organizations with gross receipts under $1 million can rely on data from three comparable organizations in similar communities.
  3. Concurrent documentation. Minutes that record the terms, the date, who voted, the data used and how any conflict was handled, written up before the next meeting or within 60 days, whichever is later.
Treas. Reg. 53.4958-6(a), (c)(2)(ii), (c)(3)

4. Pay must be clearly labeled as pay

A payment to an insider counts as pay only if RTI treats it as pay when it's made: a W-2, payroll records, a written agreement approved in advance. Anything paid informally (cash, Venmo, "reimbursing" costs that weren't really costs, or a cut of ticket money) is automatically an excess benefit, at any amount.

IRC 4958(c)(1)(A); Treas. Reg. 53.4958-4(c)

5. Employees, not contractors

A corporate officer who performs services and is paid for them is an employee for payroll-tax purposes. Running weekly classes and producing shows under the board's direction is employee work. Pay through payroll with a W-2, not a 1099. The contractor route only fits a genuinely separate business selling a defined service to many clients, and it is still a conflict-of-interest transaction.

IRC 3121(d)(1); Treas. Reg. 31.3121(d)-1(b)

6. Payroll taxes and filings

  • Withhold federal income tax and pay Social Security and Medicare (7.65% from the employee and 7.65% from RTI). File Form 941 each quarter, and W-2s and W-3 each January.
  • A 501(c)(3) is exempt from federal unemployment tax (FUTA), so there's no Form 940.
IRC 3101, 3111, 3402; IRC 3306(c)(8)

7. Form 990 reporting

  • Paid officers are listed with their pay on Form 990 or 990-EZ (Part VII), which the public can see. A 990-N postcard (gross receipts normally $50,000 or less) doesn't show pay, but the AG report below does.
  • Form 990 Part VI asks whether the pay process was independent and documented. With a 3-person board, two of them paid, the honest answer is weak; with 5 directors and a compensation committee, it's strong.
  • Any excess benefit transaction is reported on Schedule L.

8. Public charity status

RTI is a 509(a)(2) public charity. Salaries don't affect the public-support test directly, but the rules above apply in full. If RTI ever became a private foundation, reasonable pay would still be allowed under the self-dealing rules.

IRC 4941(d)(2)(E)

Federal labor law

  • Hourly is simplest. A part-time role paid by the hour is "non-exempt": keep timesheets and pay overtime past 40 hours a week. A salaried "exempt" role needs executive or administrative duties and at least $684 a week ($35,568 a year), which a grant-funded part-time role is unlikely to meet.
  • No unpaid extra hours doing the paid work. Once Stephen or Paul is paid to produce Full Spectrum, they can't "volunteer" more hours doing the same job for RTI. Those hours are owed wages. Board service itself (meetings, votes, governance) stays unpaid and separate.
Fair Labor Standards Act, 29 U.S.C. 206, 207, 213(a)(1); 29 CFR 541.600

The structure that makes it work

Governance

  • Board of five. Stephen, Paul and Albert, plus at least two independent directors: no family, business or employment ties to Stephen or Paul, and paid nothing by RTI. Respected South Shore or arts-education people also strengthen every grant.
  • Compensation committee of independent directors only (Albert and the new directors). It sets pay, approves timesheets and payroll for Stephen and Paul, and reviews pay once a year.
  • Bylaws. No change is required. Art. V §7 only makes board service unpaid, and Art. XII §1 allows reasonable pay for services. A short clarifying amendment is optional (see above).
  • Policies adopted by the board: an updated conflict-of-interest policy with annual disclosure forms, a compensation policy that follows the three safe-harbor steps, and a whistleblower policy (already drafted for the DJ Lab).

"A possible role": contingent on funding

  • Each offer letter says the position is funded only by grants or gifts that include a personnel line, and that hours go up or down with that funding.
  • No wages build up without cash. Don't let anyone work as staff now on the promise of back pay. Until a role is funded and approved, the work is volunteer board work, and it should be described that way.
  • Build personnel lines into budgets before they're submitted. The Night Out budget already lists "Project director" as $2,400 in-kind. To pay Stephen from it, change it to a cash line before Nov. 1. CPD's ARCS Service Provider partnership has no money changing hands, so it can't fund salaries itself.

Setting the rate

  • Collect at least three comparables: Form 990s of small Chicago arts or youth-music nonprofits (free on ProPublica's Nonprofit Explorer), Bureau of Labor Statistics wage data for the Chicago metro area for similar jobs (program directors, producers, operations coordinators), and nonprofit salary surveys such as Forefront's or Candid's.
  • Set an hourly rate inside that range, scaled for part-time hours. Record the sources, the rate and the vote in the minutes.
  • Don't use a percentage of grants raised or ticket revenue. Commission-style pay for fundraising raises inurement questions and violates professional fundraising ethics codes.

Accounting

  • Payroll service (Gusto, ADP or similar) handles withholding, 941s, IL-941s, W-2s and new-hire reports. Pay from an RTI account only.
  • Chart of accounts: Salaries and wages; Payroll taxes; Workers' comp and benefits. Keep these separate from Artist fees and Contract services, so officer pay never shows up as a vendor payment.
  • Program classes: Full Spectrum split into DJ Lab, Rainbow Wednesdays, stage shows, festival days and family parties. Every payroll hour is coded to one of them.
  • Timesheets with program codes, approved by the compensation committee. They prove the work happened, support grant reports, and divide salaries across program, management and fundraising for the 990's functional expense statement (Part IX).
  • Restricted funds: track each grant as restricted, and charge salary to it only if its budget includes personnel. Pay from general funds only when the board's budget allows it.
  • Monthly financial report to the full board, reviewed by an independent director: budget against actual spending, payroll totals, and cash on hand against the next payroll.
  • Expense reimbursement under a written accountable plan (receipts within 60 days). It stays separate from pay and is never used in place of pay.

How this strengthens the proposal

  • It turns a music program into youth development. Full Spectrum is pitched as music, culture and the park. A licensed counselor running a mentoring curriculum built for ages 15 to 20 adds the outcome funders ask about first: young people making better decisions.
  • It opens new funders. Youth mental-health, mentoring and violence-prevention money that a music-only program can't reach. The ask stays the same program, with a stronger reason to fund it.
  • It fits what the Lab already believes. The DJ Lab treats a crashed mix as “Data!”, not failure. S.I.P. says change your thinking, then your decisions. The same idea, on the decks and in life.
  • It gives us measurable outcomes. A short before-and-after reflection on decision-making, alongside attendance and skills, makes every grant report stronger.
  • Credibility with the young people we most want to reach. A mentor who has lived the hard path, and holds a counseling license, is a recognized model in youth work. Use it only as described in the guardrails.
  • Keep it modest. One mentoring thread inside the program, not a new clinical service. Same rule as the Bongo Beach boundary: ask for what we can defend.
