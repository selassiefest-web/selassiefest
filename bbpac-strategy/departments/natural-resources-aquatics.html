Section 1 of 8

Why This Deck Exists

This module is prepared specifically for Natural Resources / Lakefront Operations and for Aquatics, the two CPD technical units best positioned to assess whether 63rd Street Beach functions as a specialized stewardship and operations complex rather than an ordinary interior segment of Jackson Park (Park No. 19, 551.52 acres). Jackson Park's other resources — golf, harbors, the Museum-area grounds, Wooded Island, the Japanese Garden, and playing fields — are managed under interior-park practices. 63rd Street Beach is a lakefront dune/shoreline/aquatic complex, and the argument this deck asks you to weigh is narrow and technical: does that complex carry environmental stewardship and beach-operations responsibilities distinct enough from interior park management to warrant a dedicated advisory body's sustained attention, alongside whatever role JPAC continues to play for the rest of the park? This deck does not ask Natural Resources or Aquatics to rule on PAC governance, park classification, or legal authority — those questions belong to Planning & Development, Law, and LCA. What we are asking of you is confined to your own technical domain and is stated plainly in Section 7.

The Dune and Shoreline Habitat Case

63rd Street Beach includes an estimated 12 acres of native dune/shoreline habitat. Campaign research describes this habitat as used by more than 200 bird species, with threatened and endangered species sightings reported at the site. These figures matter to the case: they are the basis for treating the beach's ecological footprint as a genuine stewardship responsibility rather than an incidental green edge to a swimming beach.

Pending verification

We are flagging this figure honestly rather than presenting it as settled fact. Before any of these figures are used in an external-facing petition, public document, or press material, we intend to substantiate them against a primary source — and we would welcome Natural Resources' help identifying whether such a source already exists internally.

Section 3 of 8

The Aquatics Operational Case

Independent of the ecological question, 63rd Street Beach operates as a designated swimming beach with its own distance swimming area, lifeguard operations, a water play feature, a non-motorized boat launch, concessions, public restrooms and showers, and parking with ADA beach access. These are functions Aquatics manages directly and that do not have an analogue elsewhere in Jackson Park's interior. Water quality and seasonality considerations — testing frequency, closure triggers, staffing ramp-up and wind-down, and the operational calendar that governs a public swimming beach on Lake Michigan — are specific to a designated bathing beach and are not part of interior park management at all. This deck is not proposing any change to those protocols; it is asking Aquatics to confirm, in your own words, what makes this beach's operations distinct.

Section 4 of 8

Why Specialized Stewardship Argues for Dedicated Advisory Attention

The contrast driving this campaign is scale versus specialization: one general advisory council is currently expected to cover 551.52 acres of highly varied Jackson Park terrain — harbors, golf, cultural/museum grounds, natural areas, playing fields, and this beach — while 63rd Street Beach alone carries its own aquatic operations, its own dune-ecosystem stewardship needs, its own historic Beach House, and (per JPAC's own published materials) its own separate management as a Special Event Venue. The argument is not that Jackson Park's current advisory council has failed the beach; JPAC's mission covers all Jackson Park users, and its record shows at least some beach-related activity. The argument is structural underrepresentation: a site with this degree of specialized aquatic and ecological responsibility benefits from advisory attention that can focus on it in a sustained, technically informed way, rather than competing for bandwidth against 550-plus acres of unrelated interior-park business at every meeting.

Section 5 of 8

Comparator: South Shore Cultural Center Advisory Council

The South Shore Cultural Center Advisory Council is offered here as a stewardship-capacity comparator, not a classification precedent. Formed in 1986 to advise CPD on operations of the Cultural Center, the council today runs active committees, programming and fundraising, volunteer activities, buildings-and-grounds advocacy, and ongoing public engagement around a specialized lakefront site. We are not citing South Shore as evidence that CPD splits parks or reclassifies park numbers — South Shore Beach sits inside South Shore Cultural Center Park (#429) under a different administrative lineage than 63rd Street Beach, and that distinction matters. What South Shore demonstrates is simpler and directly relevant to this deck's audience: dedicated, sustained advisory attention to one specialized lakefront site can produce real stewardship, maintenance advocacy, and programming outcomes that a general park-wide council covering many disparate resources may not have the bandwidth to sustain.

From Aquatics: a technical statement on the specialized nature of beach operations — swimming-area management, lifeguard staffing, water quality/seasonality protocols, and how those responsibilities compare operationally to a facility that is not a designated bathing beach.

Neither ask requires a position on PAC governance, park classification, or CPD policy. Those determinations sit with Planning & Development, Law, and LCA; this deck asks only for the technical record your units are uniquely positioned to speak to.

Wouldn't a dedicated PAC create competing stewardship priorities with citywide dune-management standards?

No, and this is worth being precise about: a PAC is a community advisory body, not an operational or regulatory authority. CPD's own environmental protocols, water-quality standards, and Natural Resources oversight would continue to govern the site regardless of which advisory council covers it. A dedicated BBPAC would advise within that existing framework, not set a competing one.

What data supports the bird-species figure and the threatened/endangered species sightings?

Honestly, not yet enough. The 200+ species figure and the threatened/endangered sightings reference come from the campaign's internal research file, and we have not yet located or attached a cited primary source — no survey, hotspot count, or CPD ecological assessment is presently on file to back the number. It is marked pending verification in Section 2 for that reason, and we are actively seeking to confirm it (or correct it) against a real source before it appears in any external-facing document.

How does this affect existing water-quality monitoring protocols?

We don't know yet, because we have not obtained beach-closure or water-quality monitoring records specific to 63rd Street Beach — that gap is listed openly in Section 6 as a FOIA target. What we can say is that this campaign is not proposing any change to monitoring protocols. Recognition of the beach as a distinct PAC jurisdiction is a governance and advisory-representation question, not a request to alter aquatics testing, closure procedures, or public-health protocols.

Does a separate PAC imply a separate maintenance or capital budget, or separate Natural Resources staffing for the dunes?

Not as far as this deck is concerned. Fiscal and staffing implications, if administrative recognition moves forward, would be a question for Budget & Management at a later stage. At this stage, Natural Resources and Aquatics are being asked only for technical validation of the distinctness of the site's responsibilities — not for a commitment of budget or personnel.

Isn't there already a dune/shoreline habitat management plan for Jackson Park as a whole that a separate PAC would fragment?

We have not located one and cannot confirm either way — this is exactly the kind of record we're asking Natural Resources to help identify. If a park-wide management plan exists, a dedicated BBPAC would still operate under it, the same as any PAC operates under CPD's environmental protocols rather than setting its own; a specialized advisory council does not carry regulatory authority to displace an existing plan.

Why use South Shore Cultural Center as a comparator instead of a dune- or beach-specific precedent?

Because we're not using it as a classification precedent at all — the Precedent Library is explicit that the South Shore Beach/Cultural Center relationship is not evidence that CPD splits or reclassifies parks, since it sits inside South Shore Cultural Center Park (#429) under a different administrative lineage. It's included here solely to show what sustained, dedicated advisory attention to one specialized lakefront site can produce operationally — committees, programming, fundraising, and buildings-and-grounds advocacy — which is a fair comparator for stewardship capacity even though it says nothing about park boundaries or numbering.
