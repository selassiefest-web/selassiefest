Why This Deck Exists — And Why It Is Not Being Sent

This briefing combines the Mayor's Office and the City Council Committee on Special Events, Cultural Affairs & Recreation into a single reserve document, because the campaign's own strategy treats both as an escalation lane rather than a Gate 0 audience. It is prepared now so it is ready if needed — not because either office is being approached today.

Held in Reserve — Not an Active Ask

Per the campaign's stakeholder routing strategy, the recommended internal pathway for Gate 0 recognition runs through CPD itself: the Park Supervisor/Area Manager and LCA, then Planning, Law, and South Region/Operations, then COO-level executive review, then the General Superintendent, then the appropriate Board route. The Mayor's Office and the City Council Committee sit outside that pathway. This deck is a standby asset, not a scheduled presentation.

The Mayor's Office — Actual Relevance

The Mayor's Office is relevant to this case, but it is not a starting point. The Mayor appoints all seven Park District Board members, subject to City Council approval, which gives the Mayor's Office an indirect but real connection to who ultimately governs CPD. Separately, CPD's Legislative & Community Affairs office works with the Mayor's Office of Intergovernmental Affairs as part of the normal course of CPD's government relations.

The campaign's own framing is explicit: the Mayor's Office becomes important if the campaign hits a significant CPD policy question or institutional resistance — it is not where a routine PAC-recognition request should begin.

Section 3 of 6

The City Council Committee — Actual Jurisdiction

The Chicago City Council Committee on Special Events, Cultural Affairs & Recreation holds jurisdiction, under City Council rules, over "all matters relating to the park system within the City" and "all matters generally affecting the Chicago Park District." That jurisdiction makes it a legitimate oversight and political forum — a place for hearings or public pressure if City involvement becomes necessary.

Importantly, the Committee does not necessarily have to approve CPD's internal classification decision about whether 63rd Street Beach can be recognized as a distinct PAC jurisdiction. That decision runs through CPD's own administrative chain (the General Superintendent, CPD's Law Department, and, if legally required, the Board of Commissioners). The Committee is an oversight body that can be engaged, not a body whose approval this campaign is currently seeking.

What Would Trigger Activating This Lane

This lane should be activated only if one or more of the following occurs:

CPD leadership does not act within a reasonable timeline after the Gate 0 dossier and requests for a written determination have been submitted through the proper internal pathway; a significant policy dispute emerges that CPD's internal process cannot resolve on its own (for example, a genuine disagreement over authority, or a determination that a formal policy change is required); or the campaign needs a public hearing or political pressure point that only the City government layer can provide.

Section 5 of 6

What Not to Ask For — Prematurely

Do Not Request Council Action Before CPD's Process Has Had a Fair Chance

Do not ask the City Council Committee to act on, or the Mayor's Office to intervene in, CPD's internal classification decision before CPD's own administrative pathway — LCA, Planning, Law, Operations, the General Superintendent, and (if required) the Board — has had a fair opportunity to work. Going to the political layer too early risks reframing a governance-and-representation question as a partisan or interdepartmental dispute, and risks burning a credible escalation option before CPD has actually failed to act.

Summary

This briefing exists so the campaign can move quickly to the City government layer if escalation becomes necessary — not to make an active ask today. Recognition of 63rd Street Beach as a distinct park/PAC jurisdiction should first be pursued through CPD's own administrative pathway, with Alderman Yancy building the governmental-sponsor and external-support lane alongside it. The Mayor's Office and the City Council Committee remain in reserve, ready to be engaged if CPD's internal process stalls or a significant policy question requires it.

When exactly should we activate this lane?

Only after CPD's internal pathway — Park Supervisor/Area Manager, LCA, Planning, Law, Operations, and the General Superintendent — has had a reasonable opportunity to respond, and specifically if CPD leadership fails to act within a reasonable timeline, a significant policy dispute arises that CPD cannot resolve internally, or a public hearing becomes necessary.

Does the Mayor have direct authority over PAC recognition?

Not directly. The Mayor appoints the seven Park District Board members, with City Council approval, and CPD's LCA coordinates with the Mayor's Office of Intergovernmental Affairs as a matter of course. But the classification decision itself runs through CPD's own administrative chain, not the Mayor's Office.

What's the risk of going to Council too early?

It risks turning an administrative governance-and-representation question into a political dispute before CPD has been given a fair chance to answer it, and it risks spending a valuable escalation option prematurely — before there is a clear record of CPD inaction or resistance to point to.

Does the Committee's jurisdiction mean it must approve the classification decision?

No. The Committee's jurisdiction over "all matters relating to the park system" and "all matters generally affecting the Chicago Park District" makes it an oversight and political forum for hearings or pressure. It does not necessarily have to approve CPD's internal decision about whether 63rd Street Beach can be recognized as a distinct PAC jurisdiction.
