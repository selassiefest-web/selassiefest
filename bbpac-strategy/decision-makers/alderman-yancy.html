Section 1 of 8

63rd Street Beach Is a 5th Ward Landmark

63rd Street Beach has historically been treated as a 5th Ward lakefront asset — not just a corner of a much larger regional park, but a place with its own identity in the life of the ward. The Beach House standing there today dates to 1919, and Chicago Park District's own account traces the site's origin to South Side residents demanding more beaches for their community. That history matters: this stretch of shoreline exists because 5th Ward residents organized for it a century ago.

Today it's also home to Bongo Beach — the drumming tradition and community gathering that gives this stretch of shoreline a cultural identity found nowhere else on the lakefront. Families who grew up going to "the beach" at 63rd Street, swimmers, drummers, block clubs, and longtime residents all recognize this place as distinctly theirs. For a lot of your constituents, 63rd Street Beach isn't a line item inside Jackson Park — it's a landmark with its own name, its own sound, and its own story.

Section 2 of 8

The Representation Gap Your Constituents Are Feeling

Right now, Chicago Park District lists 63rd Street Beach as part of Jackson Park (Park No. 19) — a 551-acre park that also includes multiple harbors, a golf course, museum-area resources, Wooded Island, the Japanese Garden, playing fields, and natural areas. Jackson Park's one registered advisory council, JPAC, is asked to speak for all of it. CPD's current guidance recognizes only one registered PAC per park, so residents who want a voice dedicated specifically to the beach are told to fold their concerns into a council covering a park more than fifteen times the size of the beach complex itself.

This isn't a question of whether Jackson Park already has representation — it does. It's a question your constituents are already asking: can one general council for a 551-acre regional park realistically give a beach this complex — with its own aquatics, cultural, historic, environmental, accessibility, and safety responsibilities — the sustained attention it needs? Comparable CPD beaches, including Lane (George) Beach Park, carry their own park number despite being a fraction of the size of 63rd Street Beach, which suggests this beach's scale and complexity make it an unusually strong case for its own dedicated council.

The Constituent Question

Not "why doesn't JPAC exist," but "why does a beach this large, this specialized, and this important to the 5th Ward have to share one voice with a golf course, a museum campus, and four other distinct facilities?"

Section 3 of 8

What Your Constituents Care About: History and Stewardship

Ask 5th Ward residents what they want protected at 63rd Street Beach and two things come up again and again: the 1919 Beach House and the beach's natural shoreline. The Beach House is a piece of South Side history — a historic bathing pavilion built because residents demanded it, and it deserves the same kind of preservation attention any historic public building in the ward would get.

The natural side is just as real to constituents, even if it's less visible day to day: roughly 12 acres of dune and shoreline habitat at the beach support more than 200 bird species, including sightings of threatened and endangered species. Environmentally minded residents, birders, and neighbors who simply value a clean, well-tended shoreline all have a stake in seeing that habitat actively stewarded rather than treated as an afterthought within a much larger park's priorities. A dedicated advisory body focused on this beach — its building and its dunes — is fundamentally an investment case: protect what's historic, protect what's ecologically valuable, and make sure both get consistent attention.

Section 4 of 8

Safety, Culture, and Accountability — From a Constituent's View

63rd Street Beach runs like a small, fully operating public facility: a designated swimming beach and distance swimming area, lifeguard operations, concessions, public restrooms and showers, a water play feature, a non-motorized boat launch, parking, and ADA beach access. Chicago Park District's own materials note that the beach and Beach House are already managed separately as a Special Event Venue — meaning the site already functions, operationally, as its own distinct facility.

For your constituents, that operational complexity translates into everyday questions: Are the restrooms and showers reliably maintained? Is lifeguard coverage adequate for a swimming beach this heavily used? Is the drumming and cultural programming that defines Bongo Beach protected and supported, not just tolerated? Is there a clear, direct place to raise a concern and know it gets tracked? A council whose full attention is this beach — not one competing priority among Jackson Park's many — is better positioned to hold those day-to-day realities accountable. That's not a claim that anything has gone wrong; it's a case that a facility this specialized deserves a governance structure built to watch it closely, and that documenting conditions systematically (rather than through scattered, individual complaints) is itself part of what a dedicated council would provide.

Section 5 of 8

This Is Administrative Reform, Not a Fight With JPAC

To be direct: this initiative is not built around attacking JPAC, and your involvement shouldn't be read that way either. JPAC's published mission is to represent all Jackson Park users, and JPAC has been active on issues touching 63rd Street Beach — including parking discussions and sponsoring or advertising beach cleanups. That record is real and deserves acknowledgment.

The case for BBPAC is structural, not personal: Jackson Park is too large, 63rd Street Beach is too specialized in its own right, and the Bongo Beach community is too distinct for adequate representation to rest on one generalized, park-wide council — no matter how well that council is run. The ask is for Chicago Park District to recognize a second, focused voice alongside JPAC, not to replace or discredit the existing one. Think of it as "two strong voices for one historic Jackson Park landscape," each covering the ground it's actually built to cover.

Section 6 of 8

Your Role: Sponsor and Champion, Not Creator

It's worth being precise about what you can and can't do here. Alderman Yancy cannot personally create a PAC — that authority sits with Chicago Park District. Under CPD Code Chapter 7, the General Superintendent has authority to classify park property and designate classifications, subject to transmission to the Board of Commissioners for approval, amendment, or rejection. No published CPD procedure yet describes exactly how a community separates a facility from an existing park number, which is itself one of the first questions this campaign needs CPD to answer in writing.

What you can do — and what makes your role valuable — is lend political weight to a request that otherwise moves only through CPD's internal administrative channels. As 5th Ward Alderman, you have standing to formally request CPD action and to support the community coalition already organizing behind this effort. That's a distinct and useful role: not deciding the outcome, but making sure the request gets the visibility and seriousness a sitting elected official can bring to it.

The Ask

Become the governmental sponsor and champion of the BBPAC recognition initiative, and formally request that Chicago Park District's leadership produce an interdepartmental determination on whether — and how — 63rd Street Beach can obtain distinct recognition for PAC purposes.

Decision Brief

Decision Requested

Become the governmental sponsor/champion of the BBPAC recognition initiative and formally request an interdepartmental CPD determination.

Authority

As 5th Ward Alderman, you have standing to formally request CPD action and to convene and support constituent advocacy. You do not have unilateral authority to create a PAC — that authority rests with Chicago Park District's General Superintendent and, where required, the Board of Commissioners.

Why Now

Community organizing around BBPAC is already underway. What the effort needs at this stage is a political champion to carry the recognition request into Chicago Park District's administrative process alongside the community coalition.

Evidence

The full evidentiary case — history, operational complexity, precedents, and community coalition status — is compiled in the master presentation. See the Global Presentation for the complete record.

Options

  • Sponsor the request now
  • Sponsor with conditions (e.g., contingent on continued community organizing benchmarks)
  • Decline and refer constituents to ward constituent services
  • Wait for additional community documentation before deciding

Recommended Option

Sponsor now, given the strength of the operational-complexity case for 63rd Street Beach and the community organizing already underway to support it.

Consequences of Action

Elevates the recognition request's visibility within Chicago Park District. Signals political support for the process without committing you to any specific classification outcome.

Consequences of Inaction

The request proceeds through CPD's administrative channels without a political champion — a real disadvantage for an initiative that depends on sustained attention across multiple CPD departments and, potentially, the Board.

Next Procedural Action

Send a sponsorship letter to Chicago Park District leadership requesting the interdepartmental determination.

What does sponsoring this actually commit me to?

It commits you to formally requesting that Chicago Park District produce a written interdepartmental determination on 63rd Street Beach's eligibility for distinct PAC recognition. It does not commit you to a specific outcome, to a specific classification mechanism, or to any position on how CPD should ultimately structure the beach's governance — those are CPD's calls to make.

Could this create tension with JPAC or other 5th Ward stakeholders?

The campaign is deliberately framed as structural underrepresentation, not as a critique of JPAC's performance — JPAC's own mission covers all of Jackson Park, and JPAC has been active on beach-related issues. Sponsoring this request is consistent with supporting two strong, coordinated voices for one shared landscape, not with taking a side against an existing community body.

What's my role if CPD says no?

Your role as sponsor is to request the determination, not to guarantee the result. If CPD's initial answer is no, the next step — consistent with the campaign's own structure — is to ask CPD in writing what specific authority or procedure stands in the way, and whether a policy change would be required. You can continue as a champion for that follow-up conversation without having promised a particular outcome up front.

Am I being asked to create a PAC myself?

No. You cannot create a PAC — that authority belongs to Chicago Park District, ultimately running through the General Superintendent and, where required, the Board of Commissioners. Your role is to sponsor the request for a determination and lend it political visibility, not to perform an administrative act that isn't within your authority.

Does this involve the beach becoming privatized or less public?

No. This is a request about community advisory governance — how residents get a dedicated voice in a Park District process — not a change in who owns, operates, or has access to the beach. The beach remains public Chicago Park District property either way.

What should I tell constituents who ask why this matters to the ward?

63rd Street Beach has long been treated as a 5th Ward lakefront asset with its own history, going back to the 1919 Beach House and residents' demand for more beaches, and its own present-day cultural identity through Bongo Beach. Supporting a dedicated advisory voice for that beach is about making sure a facility this important to the ward gets governance attention proportional to what it actually is — not about diminishing Jackson Park's existing council.
