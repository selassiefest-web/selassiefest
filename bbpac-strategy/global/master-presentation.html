Section 1 of 20

Executive Summary & the Decision Requested

63rd Street Beach — including its 1919 Beach House and surrounding grounds — is currently administered by the Chicago Park District (CPD) as part of Jackson Park, designated Park No. 19. Jackson Park already has a registered Park Advisory Council, the Jackson Park Advisory Council (JPAC). CPD's February 2025 PAC Guidelines recognize only one registered PAC per park. As a result, an ordinary request to form a "63rd Street Bongo Beach PAC" (BBPAC) can be rejected on classification grounds alone, without CPD ever reaching the merits of whether the beach needs and deserves dedicated representation.

This presentation is the master evidentiary case behind that request. It does not ask CPD to approve a PAC outright. It asks CPD to resolve a threshold classification question — what this campaign refers to internally as Gate 0 — before PAC formation can even be attempted. Three outcomes are being requested, in descending order of preference:

  • Outcome A (preferred) — Distinct park status. CPD recognizes or designates 63rd Street Beach, its Beach House, and associated defined grounds as a distinct park unit for PAC purposes.
  • Outcome B — Formal exception or interpretation. CPD determines that a sufficiently distinct major facility within a very large park may have its own registered facility-specific PAC, notwithstanding the existing Jackson Park PAC.
  • Outcome C — Policy change. If current policy permits neither A nor B, CPD amends its policy to accommodate unique large-park facilities such as major beaches, cultural centers, and other independently operated complexes.

What is being asked of CPD at this stage

Not approval of a PAC. A determination — through whichever office or process CPD identifies as the correct one — as to whether 63rd Street Beach can be recognized as a distinct administrative unit for PAC purposes, and if so, how. Section 10 of this presentation sets out that request in full.

History & Geography of 63rd Street Beach

63rd Street Beach sits within Jackson Park, a 551.52-acre park with an unusually wide range of facilities: multiple harbors, a golf operation, resources associated with the museum campus, Wooded Island, the Japanese Garden, playing fields, natural areas, and 63rd Street Beach itself. Jackson Park is one of the largest and most functionally diverse parks in the CPD system, and this scale is central to the argument developed in later sections.

The beach's own history predates its current administrative treatment as one line item within that larger park. CPD's own description of the site attributes its origin to South Side residents demanding more beach access, and the historic 1919 Beach House — a bathing pavilion built to serve that demand — remains standing and in public use today. Partially confirmed Further archival detail on the Beach House's construction history and the specific advocacy that produced it has not yet been fully assembled from primary CPD records and is part of the planned records-request effort described elsewhere in this campaign.

Jackson Park's own administrative lineage is itself a precedent for administrative change: the park was created by subdividing a much larger predecessor parcel. South Park Commission subdivision (1880–81) is the relevant reference point — Jackson Park did not always exist in its current form, and CPD's predecessor agency reorganized park boundaries to create it.

Bongo Beach: Cultural Identity and Community History

63rd Street Beach carries a distinct cultural identity in the community that has given rise to its popular name, "Bongo Beach" — a reference to a long-standing drumming tradition associated with the site and the community history that has grown up around it. This cultural identity is one of the reasons the beach is experienced by many South Side residents as its own place, separate in character from the rest of Jackson Park, rather than as one recreational amenity among many within a 551-acre regional park.

Section 4 of 20

Current CPD Classification & the Gate 0 Problem

CPD's records presently list 63rd Street Beach as located within Jackson Park, Park No. 19. Jackson Park already has a registered Park Advisory Council, JPAC. CPD's February 2025 PAC Guidelines state a clear rule governing this situation:

CPD PAC Guidelines, February 2025

"The Park District recognizes only one (1) registered PAC per park."

Where a park already has a PAC, CPD's standard instruction to residents is to join the existing council. Applied literally to 63rd Street Beach, this rule allows CPD to reject an ordinary Letter of Intent for a "BBPAC" in a single sentence: the beach is in Jackson Park; Jackson Park already has a PAC. No amount of community organizing, programming vision, or bylaws drafting changes that outcome if the classification question is never addressed first.

This is why the campaign treats classification, not PAC formation, as the first problem to solve — referred to throughout this presentation as Gate 0. Sections 5 through 9 build the evidentiary case for why 63rd Street Beach warrants a different classification treatment; Section 10 sets out the specific outcomes being requested.

Correction to an earlier assumption

An earlier working assumption in this campaign held that PACs might be subject to the Illinois Open Meetings Act. Current CPD guidance states that PACs are not subject to the Open Meetings Act. That earlier assumption was incorrect and is not carried forward in this presentation or the campaign's other materials.

Section 5 of 20

The Operational-Complexity Case

The core argument of this presentation is not that Jackson Park lacks any representation. It is that one general advisory council for a 551-acre regional park cannot realistically provide adequate governance attention to a major, independently operated historic beach complex with its own aquatics, cultural, environmental, historic, accessibility, safety, and programming responsibilities. 63rd Street Beach is not a small playground inside Jackson Park. It is, functionally, a distinct complex with its own list of facilities and operations:

  • A separately managed Special Event Venue — an administrative fact acknowledged on JPAC's own website (developed further in Section 6)
  • The historic 1919 Beach House, a bathing pavilion with distinct public functions
  • A designated swimming beach and a distance swimming area
  • Lifeguard operations
  • Concessions
  • Public restrooms and showers
  • A water play feature
  • A non-motorized boat launch
  • Parking and ADA beach access
  • Approximately 12 acres of specialized dune and shoreline habitat (detailed in Section 7)
  • Significant, ongoing cultural use tied to the Bongo Beach drumming tradition (detailed in Section 3)

No other single facility inside Jackson Park combines this particular set of aquatics, historic-preservation, accessibility, environmental, and cultural responsibilities. That combination is the operational basis for treating the beach as a distinct unit for representation purposes.

Distinct Identity & Operational Independence

The single strongest piece of evidence for administrative distinctness comes from JPAC itself, not from this campaign. JPAC's own website states:

JPAC website

"63rd Street Beach and Beachhouse is managed separately as a Special Event Venue."

This is a significant admission. It means the entity CPD currently treats as the beach's sole advisory voice already describes the beach as managed on a separate operational track from the rest of Jackson Park. That separation is not something this campaign is proposing for the first time — it is an existing administrative reality that has not yet been matched with a corresponding governance structure.

This is the basis for a planned standalone document in the Gate 0 recognition dossier, the Distinct Identity & Operational Independence Brief, which will develop this point with supporting operational detail (event permitting, revenue treatment, and facility-management practice) beyond what is presented here.

A useful modern analogy for what "distinct but still nested" can look like administratively is Maggie Daley Park — a distinctly named, operationally independent "park within a park" that remains officially within Grant Park. It illustrates that CPD already has a working model for recognizing a major facility's distinct identity without severing it entirely from its parent park.

Environmental Stewardship at 63rd Street Beach

63rd Street Beach includes approximately 12 acres of specialized dune and shoreline habitat. This habitat is used by more than 200 bird species, and sightings of threatened and endangered species have been recorded there. This is a substantial ecological resource located within a single beach complex, requiring stewardship expertise — dune management, shoreline protection, habitat monitoring — that is distinct from the recreational and cultural functions described elsewhere in this presentation.

This environmental profile is the basis for a planned standalone Beach-Specific Stewardship Report in the Gate 0 recognition dossier, and it is also the reason CPD's Natural Resources and Lakefront Operations functions are identified elsewhere in this campaign as technical validators of the case, separate from the PAC-governance question itself. Environmental stewardship needs, on their own, do not determine whether a facility should have its own PAC — but they add a further, independent dimension to why 63rd Street Beach functions as more than a generic recreational amenity within a 551-acre park.

Section 8 of 20

Framing Discipline: This Is Not an Attack on JPAC

JPAC's published mission is to represent all users of Jackson Park, and JPAC's own records show at least some engagement with 63rd Street Beach — including discussion of parking changes and sponsorship or advertising of beach cleanups. This campaign does not argue that JPAC has failed or neglected the beach. That claim has not been established, and this presentation does not make it.

The argument being made instead is structural, not personal: Jackson Park is too large, 63rd Street Beach is too specialized, and the Bongo Beach community is too distinct for adequate representation to depend on a single generalized park-wide council. A planned Representation Gap Analysis — reviewing JPAC's recent agendas, minutes, financial reports, committees, and activities against the scale and needs of the beach — is intended to test and document this claim before it is asserted more strongly than the evidence supports. The likely finding, based on current information, is structural underrepresentation rather than absence of any representation at all.

What not to do

  • Do not submit an ordinary CPD Letter of Intent titled "63rd Street Bongo Beach PAC" without first addressing the classification premise described in Section 4. CPD can reject that letter in one sentence under the current framing.
  • Do not confuse naming with jurisdiction. CPD has a separate, independent procedure for naming parks and features, involving public support and Board consideration. Winning an official "Bongo Beach" name would not, by itself, create a separate park for PAC purposes. Naming may become a parallel project later, but it is not a substitute for resolving Gate 0.
  • Do not build this campaign around the premise that JPAC failed. Frame every JPAC-facing communication around coordination and structural underrepresentation, not fault.
Section 9 of 20

Legal & Administrative Authority Basis

CPD Code Chapter 7 gives the General Superintendent authority to classify park property and designate classifications, with those classification decisions transmitted to the Board of Commissioners for approval, amendment, or rejection. The Board's own governing language is direct on where ultimate control resides:

CPD Code

"The management and control of business and property of the Park District shall be vested in the Board of Commissioners."

Beyond this general framework, however, the precise legal mechanism by which a community could separate a facility such as 63rd Street Beach from an existing park number and obtain distinct recognition has not been identified. Pending verification No published CPD procedure describing this specific process has been found to date. This is one of the first written questions this campaign intends to put to CPD's Law Department: who has the authority to make this determination, and what procedure applies. This presentation does not presume an answer to that question, and no department- or decision-maker-facing material generated from this master presentation should either.

What is established, and can be stated with confidence: the General Superintendent has classification authority under Chapter 7, and any resulting classification action would go to the Board of Commissioners, where four affirmative votes are required to carry a motion. What remains open is whether a facility-specific PAC recognition, short of a full park-number split, requires that same Board-level action, or can be accomplished through an administrative determination alone. That is a question for CPD's Law Department, not for this campaign to resolve on its own.

Gate 0 — the Three Requested Outcomes

Sections 4 through 9 establish why the ordinary PAC-formation process fails at the first step for 63rd Street Beach, and why the beach's operational, cultural, and environmental profile supports a different classification treatment. This section states, in full, what is actually being requested of CPD to resolve that threshold problem.

Outcome A (preferred) — Distinct park status

CPD recognizes or designates 63rd Street Beach, its Beach House, and associated defined grounds as a distinct park unit for PAC purposes. This is the cleanest outcome and the one with the most direct precedent support: smaller, simpler beaches already carry independent park numbers within the CPD system. Lane Beach Park #1265 is the clearest example — a 3.09-acre beach maintained as its own numbered park, far smaller and operationally simpler than 63rd Street Beach.

Outcome B — Formal exception or interpretation

If CPD determines that reclassifying the beach into a fully distinct park number is not warranted or feasible, CPD could instead determine that a sufficiently distinct major facility within a very large park may have its own registered facility-specific PAC, notwithstanding the existing Jackson Park PAC. This would not require redrawing Park No. 19's boundaries — only an administrative interpretation that the "one PAC per park" rule accommodates a facility of this scale and distinctness.

Outcome C — Policy change

If CPD's Law Department determines that current policy permits neither A nor B, the request becomes a request to amend that policy — to create a defined path by which unique large-park facilities (major beaches, cultural centers, or other independently operated complexes elsewhere in the CPD system) can obtain dedicated advisory representation in the future, not only for 63rd Street Beach.

Primary ask

Direct the appropriate CPD departments to establish a defined administrative status for the 63rd Street Beach complex that permits dedicated PAC representation, and submit any necessary action to the Board of Commissioners. Pending verification The exact wording of this ask is subject to revision once CPD's Law Department clarifies which of Outcomes A, B, or C is actually available and what authority governs the decision — this presentation states the ask as currently understood, not as a final legal position.

Formal Request for Written CPD Determination

Because CPD's own guidelines permit an ordinary Letter of Intent to be rejected in a single sentence — 63rd Street Beach sits inside Jackson Park No. 19, and Jackson Park already has a registered PAC — the campaign's foundational move is not a PAC application. It is a written request asking CPD to resolve the classification question on the record, before any Letter of Intent is filed. CPD Code Chapter 7 gives the General Superintendent authority to classify park property, subject to transmittal to the Board for approval, amendment, or rejection, but no published CPD procedure describes how a community separates a facility from an existing park number and obtains a new one. That gap is itself one of the questions CPD must answer.

Six Questions CPD Should Answer in Writing

(a) Does CPD consider 63rd Street Beach eligible for independent PAC representation?

(b) If not, is the reason solely its current inclusion in Jackson Park No. 19?

(c) What officer or body has authority to designate 63rd Street Beach as a distinct park or administrative park unit?

(d) What procedure is required to obtain such a designation?

(e) Can the General Superintendent, LCA, PAC Governance Committee, or Board approve a facility-specific PAC?

(f) If current policy prohibits it, what process is required to seek a policy amendment?

These six questions are the spine of the entire campaign. Every department presentation, every precedent citation, and every coalition letter exists to give CPD the factual basis to answer them — not to argue CPD into a predetermined outcome.

The Precedent Case, Summarized

The full Precedent Library, with sourcing and confirmation status for every entry, lives at the Precedent Library. This deck does not re-derive that research; it points to it. The Library is organized around two questions, plus one open research thread.

Bucket A — can CPD change or reorganize existing park identity? South Park's 1880–81 subdivision shows Jackson Park's own administrative lineage begins with a split, not a fixed original boundary. Maggie Daley Park shows a modern, operationally independent "park within a park" that remains officially inside Grant Park — the cleanest modern reclassification precedent. Seneca Park #1242 shows land carved out of a larger park and given its own number and its own PAC. Rutherford Sayre Park's 1999 consolidation shows CPD reorganizing park units in the opposite direction from BBPAC's ask, which proves the mechanism exists even though this instance runs the other way. Recent boundary transfers at Dooley Park and Washington Park show CPD boundaries are not fixed or historically untouchable.

Bucket B — can a beach itself be an independent numbered CPD park? Lane Beach Park #1265 is the lead comparator: at only 3.09 acres it carries its own park number, while the far larger and more complex 63rd Street Beach does not. Chase Avenue Beach, Park #1250 is the strongest candidate resembling a large-park-to-separately-numbered-beach split, though the separation year is Partially confirmed and still requires a Board or property-file record. A dozen-plus other CPD beaches — Hartigan, Doria, Fargo, Howard, and others — already exist as independently numbered parks. South Shore Beach is a facility inside South Shore Cultural Center Park #429 and is not evidence that CPD splits parks; it is discussed on its own terms in the next section.

The open research thread is the Park-Number Administrative Paper Trail, a seven-question investigation into how CPD creates, splits, merges, and retires park numbers. Its seventh question — a confirmed post-1934 CPD action splitting one park number into two — is Pending verification and remains the single highest-priority open research item in the entire campaign.

South Shore Cultural Center: The Comparative Case Study

South Shore Cultural Center Advisory Council is not offered to CPD as a precedent for splitting a park. Its own beach, South Shore Beach, sits inside South Shore Cultural Center Park #429 and does not share Jackson Park's administrative lineage — it is a comparator, not a classification precedent, and this deck treats it accordingly.

What South Shore Cultural Center demonstrates is what a dedicated, specialized lakefront PAC can actually accomplish once recognized. Its advisory council was formed in 1986 to advise CPD on operations of the Cultural Center, promote maintenance and beautification, and develop cultural, recreational, and educational activities. Today it runs standing committees, public meetings, fundraising, volunteer activities, advocacy, and community engagement — the operating model BBPAC proposes to follow for 63rd Street Beach.

The full comparative deck prepared for this constituency lives at the South Shore Cultural Center Advisory Council presentation. Section 18 of 20

The Four-Phase Structure

The campaign is sequenced in four phases, and each phase depends on the one before it succeeding.

Phase 0 — Win the right to form BBPAC. This is Gate 0: securing one of Outcome A, B, or C from CPD. Nothing in Phases 1 through 3 can begin until Phase 0 is resolved.

Phase 1 — Form BBPAC. Once eligible, the campaign follows CPD's ordinary PAC-formation process: meet with CPD, submit a Letter of Intent, convene a properly noticed community meeting, complete membership applications, nominate officers, conduct elections with CPD representation present, adopt bylaws, register as a nonprofit, and submit the New PAC Registration.

Phase 2 — Obtain CPD recognition. Formal registration of BBPAC as a recognized CPD PAC.

Phase 3 — Operate the gold-standard beach PAC. Ongoing PAC binder maintenance and annual compliance documentation, per CPD requirements, on the model demonstrated by South Shore Cultural Center's advisory council.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the difference between park classification, PAC governance, and PAC recognition?

Classification is CPD's administrative act of defining what a park unit is and where its boundaries lie, under the General Superintendent's Code Chapter 7 authority. PAC governance refers to the rules a registered PAC must follow once it exists, including the Code of Conduct matters handled by the PAC Governance Committee. PAC recognition is CPD's act of registering a specific advisory council for a specific park unit. BBPAC's problem is at the classification layer: without a distinct classification for 63rd Street Beach, the recognition question never gets reached, because the existing rule directs any new PAC request back to JPAC.

Why does "Gate 0" need to happen before anything else?

Because CPD's published PAC Guidelines state that the Park District recognizes only one registered PAC per park, and 63rd Street Beach is currently listed within Jackson Park No. 19, which already has JPAC. An ordinary Letter of Intent for a "BBPAC" can be rejected in one sentence under that framing. Gate 0 is the petition that asks CPD to resolve the underlying classification question first, so that a Letter of Intent — Phase 1 — has somewhere to land.

What happens if CPD says no to all three outcomes — A, B, and C?

The source material does not specify a defined next step for that scenario. Pending verification. What is documented is the broader pathway available if CPD's administrative process reaches an impasse: the City Council Committee on Special Events, Cultural Affairs & Recreation has jurisdiction over matters affecting the park system and can serve as an oversight or political forum, and the Mayor's Office becomes relevant if the campaign hits significant policy resistance, since the Mayor appoints the seven Park District Board members. Neither is described in the source as an automatic escalation, and both sit outside CPD's own administrative process.

How long is this expected to take?

No timeline is stated anywhere in the source material, and none should be implied. Pending verification. The sequencing is defined — records requests, dossier assembly, department review, executive review, then a Board route if one is legally required — but no duration commitments from CPD exist yet.

Does this take authority away from JPAC?

No. The campaign is deliberately built around a "Two Strong Voices, One Historic Jackson Park Landscape" framing, not a transfer of authority away from JPAC. JPAC's consent is never treated as a requirement for BBPAC's recognition unless CPD states that it legally is one. The campaign's sought outcomes with JPAC, in descending order, are support, neutrality or no objection, or at minimum a documented opportunity to comment.

Is this privatization of the beach?

No. Nothing in the source material proposes transferring ownership, management, or operations of 63rd Street Beach out of the Chicago Park District. BBPAC, like every CPD PAC, would be an advisory council to CPD, not an operator or owner.

Will 63rd Street Beach remain public?

Yes. The beach remains a public CPD facility under this proposal. The only thing under discussion is which advisory body represents the community on beach-related matters and how CPD classifies the property for that purpose.

Does this cost CPD money, or require a new budget line?

That is not yet resolved. Pending verification. The source material identifies Budget & Management as a CPD office that "becomes important once an administrative structure is contemplated," to determine whether separate administrative recognition carries fiscal or cost-center consequences — but no figures or budget determinations exist in the record yet.

What would a CPD official actually be agreeing to by supporting Outcome A, B, or C — and what are they not being asked to do?

They would be agreeing to direct the appropriate CPD departments to establish a defined administrative status for the 63rd Street Beach complex permitting dedicated PAC representation, and to submit any necessary action to the Board of Commissioners if Board approval is legally required. They are not being asked to approve BBPAC's bylaws, elections, or membership today — that is Phase 1, a separate and later step — and they are not being asked to remove JPAC's PAC registration or authority over the rest of Jackson Park.

Does winning an official "Bongo Beach" name accomplish this?

No. CPD's park- and feature-naming procedure, which runs on public support and Board consideration, is separate from the park-classification and PAC-recognition process this campaign is pursuing. A name change would not by itself create a distinct park unit or unlock PAC eligibility. Naming may be pursued as a parallel project later, but it is not a substitute for Gate 0.

Who inside CPD can actually say yes to this?

No single office decides alone. The Park Supervisor and Area Manager and LCA are the entry point for formation questions; Planning & Development documents the boundary and classification mechanism; the Law Department determines what legal instrument, if any, is required and whether Board approval is necessary; the Chief Operating Officer assesses operational viability; and the General Superintendent sits at the center of the administrative decision chain, with Board action following if legally required. The PAC Governance Committee is explicitly not the body that decides this — CPD's published materials describe that committee principally in connection with PAC complaints and Code of Conduct disputes.

Is this an accusation that JPAC neglected the beach?

No, and the campaign is written to avoid that framing entirely. JPAC's published mission covers all Jackson Park users, and its records show some engagement with the beach, including parking discussions and cleanup sponsorships. The argument is structural underrepresentation — one general council for a 551-acre park is not automatically sufficient governance for a facility of this scale and complexity — supported by a documented Representation Gap Analysis rather than an allegation.

What is the Gate 0 Recognition Dossier, and why sixteen documents?

It is the complete evidentiary and organizational package the campaign is assembling to support the Gate 0 petition — covering the legal petition itself, boundary definition, operational-independence evidence, history and culture, environmental stewardship, documented conditions, the representation-gap analysis, the South Shore comparator, the precedent set, community support, endorsements, official letters, a leadership roster, and BBPAC's own proposed mission and bylaws. Sixteen documents are needed because the petition has to satisfy legal, historical, environmental, operational, and community-credibility standards simultaneously — a one-page letter cannot carry that weight on its own. Live status of each document is tracked at the evidence status page.

Has the FOIA/records campaign been filed yet?

No. As of this presentation, no public-records request in this campaign has been filed. Pending verification. The planned categories of records are defined and listed at the evidence status page, but the requests themselves remain future work.

What role does Alderman Yancy play, and can he create BBPAC himself?

No alderman can create a CPD PAC; that is a CPD administrative process, not a City Council act. The 5th Ward has historically treated 63rd Street Beach as a lakefront asset, and the ask of Alderman Yancy is to become the governmental sponsor and champion of the recognition initiative and to request an interdepartmental CPD determination — a political and advocacy role that runs alongside CPD's process, not a substitute for it.

Does South Shore Cultural Center's advisory council prove CPD can split a park the way BBPAC is asking?

No. South Shore Beach sits inside South Shore Cultural Center Park #429 and does not share Jackson Park's administrative lineage, so it is explicitly not used in this campaign as a classification precedent — see the Precedent Library's discussion of that distinction. It is used only to show what a dedicated, specialized lakefront PAC can accomplish once one exists.
